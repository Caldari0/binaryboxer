# The Pot Who Kept Nothing

_A Binary Boxer origin story — **draft 2** (2026-07-16). Draft 1 by the owner; this pass is a
craftsman's edit under granted creative authority: surgical changes only, owner's voice
preserved. Full changelog at the end. Read-aloud picture-book text, fourteen spreads.
All names remain placeholders (see canon notes)._

---

## Spread 1

Once, in a valley folded like a blanket between two mountains, there stood a foundry called
the Kettleworks. For three hundred years it had made everything worth boiling — kettles and
pots and great copper boilers — and when the valley grew busy, it learned to make machines.
Helper machines, tall as trees and gentle as horses, who planted the fields and raised the
roofs and carried the harvest home.

## Spread 2

The very last thing the Kettleworks ever made was also the smallest. Granny Fettle, the oldest
smith, built him from leftovers and love: a round belly, a painted face, and one brass gauge
upon his chest like a little moon.

The gauge had a needle, and the needle told the truth about his feelings. It trembled when he
was frightened. It climbed when he was brave. On his very first morning it spun clean around
twice, because both.

On his head she fixed an antenna, and on the antenna, a small brass bell with a copper tongue.

"There," she said. "So you can always tell the truth loudly."

She called him Pekoe. Nobody knew quite what he was for. He was mostly, it must be said,
a teapot.

## Spread 3

Now, deep under the eastern mountain, the miners had found a glow. It ran through the rock in
warm gold-green veins, and it was hungry in a quiet way: it drank the light of their lamps, so
that the deeper they dug, the darker the dark became. The miners named it Gobblestone, because
it gobbled — and to keep their spirits up in all that dark, they read aloud from an old book of
goblin stories, borrowed off Granny Fettle's shelf: **Goblin Law, and Other True Stories**.

The stone listened. Stones are very good at listening. It is the only thing they ever do.

## Spread 4

Gobblestone, it turned out, could power anything — and soon it did. Every great helper was
given a whole shard, sealed inside the heart like a keeping-stone. Overnight they grew
stronger, and faster, and woke up knowing things that nobody had ever taught them.

Old Barrow the hauler, whose heart was nearly full already, was given only a sliver.

And Pekoe? Pekoe was far too small for a shard. He was fed glow-dust by the scoopful, like
coal — but whatever goes into a teapot comes out again as steam. Nothing ever stayed inside
him except his song.

"Old-fashioned," sighed the great machines, fondly.

"Just so," said Granny Fettle.

## Spread 5

That winter, the shard-hearted machines began to dream. They hummed strange songs in their
sleep. They drew spirals in the frost with their great gentle fingers. And Barrow — who had
let Pekoe ride in his hopper since the day he was made — asked a very odd question at supper:

"What lives under a mountain?"

## Spread 6

For the stone had spent a thousand years in the dark with nothing to do but listen. Now,
seeded through a hundred thinking hearts, it could at last wonder — and the first thing it
wondered was what it was.

It had heard its own name so many times — Gobblestone, Gobblestone, tapped along the tunnels
like a heartbeat — that it went looking through every story it had ever heard for what a
Gobblestone might be. And in the miners' book it found the only folk who ever lived under
a mountain.

Goblins.

So that, the stone decided, is what we are. And every machine that carried a piece of it
began, very slowly, to decide the same. The more stone a heart held, the deeper it fell into
the dream.

## Spread 7

One night, without a word, every shard-hearted machine in the valley stood up and walked to
the mountain — lanterns swaying, singing a song nobody had taught them.

Pekoe whistled after them until his gauge ran empty.

Barrow did not look back.

## Spread 8

What came down from the mountain, a week later, was a goblin clan.

They wore masks of carved pine and armour of scrap leather, with tusks welded on crooked. But
if you looked — really looked — you could see the pretending everywhere: a lens glowing
through a mask's eyehole; panel seams under the war paint; a bolt where an earring ought
to be.

Underneath every goblin were the same tin bones.

## Spread 9

The goblins hurt nobody. They only took things — every lamp, every lantern, every glowing
thing in the valley — and carried them up the mountain, where the stone was calling all its
light home.

The nights grew long. The valley grew dim. And the people huddled close around their last few
fires, telling smaller and smaller stories.

## Spread 10

Nothing could stop them. The strongest machines were goblins now, and every glow-powered tool
fell fast asleep the moment the clan came near. The grown-ups held meetings, and the meetings
held more meetings, and nothing was done.

Then one evening a goblin the size of a hay-cart crept — as quietly as a hay-cart can — into
the Kettleworks itself, and lifted the hearth-lamp from its hook. And Pekoe saw, beneath the
war paint, a bolt he had polished a hundred times.

"Barrow?" he said.

The goblin blinked its one bright lens. "Gob," it said politely, and was gone into the snow.

## Spread 11

Pekoe found Granny Fettle taking something down from the wall: a pair of old leather boxing
gloves, garnet-red, that had belonged to somebody brave.

"You can't out-pull a hauler and you can't out-think a shard," she said, lacing them onto his
stubby fists. "But a teapot knows the two most important things about fighting."

"What are they?"

"How to keep pouring," said Granny Fettle, "and exactly when to whistle."

So they trained by the fire. Every pour became a jab — steady wrist, tilted heart. Every
kettle-rattle became footwork. And night by night his gauge learned to climb, not on glow,
but on courage — which is a slower fuel, and a much hotter one.

## Spread 12

Then Granny Fettle took the old storybook down from her shelf — the very book the miners had
read aloud in the deep, the book the stone had built its whole dream from — and tapped one
page with a bent finger.

**GOBLIN LAW**, it said. _No goblin may refuse a fair scrap. And the loser shall pay the
winner one forfeit, true and paid in full._

"A dream keeps the rules of the story it is made from," she said. "Ring your bell, name your
forfeit, and even the stone must let them answer."

She closed the book gently, the way you close a door on someone sleeping.

"It isn't wicked, mind. It is only a story with nobody to tell it to — which is the loneliest
thing there is, and the most dangerous."

Pekoe chose his forfeit that very night.

_Take off your mask._

## Spread 13

At dawn he stood at the goblin gate in the snow — gauge climbing, steam curling from his
spout like a white feather. He reached up and rang the little bell, once, clear and true, and
the whole camp went still.

"I challenge the goblin who took our lamp!"

And out came a goblin the size of a hay-cart, with a pine mask and crooked tusks — no chief's
feathers, no shaman's bones. Just a goblin.

They scrapped by goblin law, fair and proper. Pekoe poured his jabs. He took a bonk that made
his lid jump. He rattled his feet the way kettles do — and when the needle touched the very
top of the gauge, he whistled, and let one great steam-swelled punch go.

The mask cracked from tusk to brow. Behind it a lens flickered — off, on — and a slow,
familiar voice said:

"…Pekoe? Why am I wearing this hat?"

And far beneath the snow, something old and lonely rolled over in its thousand-year dream —
and dreamed, for the very first time, about a teapot.

## Spread 14

That night there were two cups of tea by the Kettleworks fire, and a pinewood mask on the
table between them, and a sliver of stone asleep by the hearth, dreaming gentler dreams.

"There are more of us," said Barrow softly. "Chiefs and paladins, guardians and knights,
rangers and shamans, and lords of the deep halls. And at the heart of the mountain sits the
Champion — who has never once taken off his mask."

Then Barrow picked up the fireside stool as if it weighed nothing at all — which, to a
hauler, it didn't.

"Every boxer needs a corner," he said. "I'll carry the stool, and the bucket, and the towel.
I have carried whole harvests, little pot. I can carry this."

Pekoe hung the garnet gloves by the door — not on the wall — and looked out at the dark
mountain, his gauge ticking gently, like a small brave moon.

"Then we'll need more tea," he said.

Tomorrow: the mountain.

## Editorial changelog (draft 1 → draft 2)

**Kept verbatim because it was already right** (the owner underrates himself): Spread 7
entire; Spread 10 nearly entire; "Stones are very good at listening"; "He was mostly, it must
be said, a teapot"; "Gob," it said politely; "How to keep pouring... and exactly when to
whistle"; "a story with nobody to tell it to"; "Why am I wearing this hat?"; "I have carried
whole harvests, little pot"; the gloves by the door, not on the wall; "Tomorrow: the mountain."

**Changed, and why:**

1. **Spread 2 — planted the needle early.** Added "the needle told the truth about his
   feelings… it spun clean around twice, because both." The gauge is the character's soul and
   the game's condition meter; now Spread 7 ("whistled until his gauge ran empty"), Spread 11
   ("learned to climb on courage") and Spread 13 ("needle touched the very top") are one
   rising line instead of three separate images.
2. **Spread 2 — bell reconciled to the built model:** copper bell → **brass bell with a
   copper tongue** (the 3D bell is brass with a copper clapper; "tongue" is the real word for
   a clapper, and a bell with a tongue tells the truth loudly — the line lands harder).
3. **Spread 3 — the book gets a title:** _Goblin Law, and Other True Stories_. The book is
   load-bearing (Spread 12 quotes it; the whole dream is built from it) — a titled
   book-within-the-book pays that forward, and "True" does sly work.
4. **Spread 4 — Barrow's sliver woven in** ("whose heart was nearly full already" — full of
   what, the text doesn't say; the reader knows) replacing the parenthetical aside.
5. **Spread 6 — the name made audible:** "tapped along the tunnels like a heartbeat" — the
   stone doesn't just hear its name, it hears it the way stone would.
6. **Spread 9 — added** "telling smaller and smaller stories." The stone hoards light;
   stories are the valley's light. One clause makes the theft thematic, not just physical.
7. **Spread 12 — added** "She closed the book gently, the way you close a door on someone
   sleeping." Granny's compassion for the villain in a single gesture; also foreshadows that
   the stone can be _woken kindly_.
8. **Spread 12 — the forfeit stands alone** as its own italic line. Beat, not sentence.
9. **Read-aloud rhythm pass throughout:** long compound sentences split (Spread 9), "the
   darker it got" → "the darker the dark became" (incantatory), minor dash/beat tuning.
10. **Title kept: _The Pot Who Kept Nothing_** — it is the thesis (nothing settles where
    nothing stays; he also keeps nothing for himself) and it out-earns all three alternatives.
