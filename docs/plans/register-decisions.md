## The writing safety net (the owner is the director, not the writer)

The fear: a good game buried under messy, incoherent dialogue. The diagnosis: tedium comes from
**volume without reactivity** (the FM-press-conference failure), not from imperfect sentences.
Undertale = tiny + reactive + rigid voice rules; Stardew = large + static. We are structurally
on the first side; these rules keep it that way:

1. **Voice cards** — every speaker gets a card: 5–8 voice rules + 3 sample lines, owner
   taste-passed once (the epithet-table workflow). Writers (AI sessions) generate against the
   card; no card, no lines. Cards needed: narrator/storyteller, Granny Fettle, the 9 goblin
   ranks (corruption style + woken voice each), 10 language flavours (epithets done ✅).
2. **Every line earns its place** — a line must do one of: explain a choice (reason), reveal
   character, or set up/pay off a joke or a tenderness. None of the three → cut.
3. **Reactivity over ambience** — lines triggered by play state outrank ambient chatter;
   ambient gets a hard budget (a handful per screen, rotated, never repeated in-session).
4. **Tedium tripwires** — skip stays always-visible (page grammar law); playtest adds the
   question "which text did you skip?" — skipped text gets cut or made reactive, never longer.
   The Gate 1 pass condition (testers *explain* outcomes) already tests the reason layer.
5. **Volume ceiling** — the whole game's text should stay small enough to voice-pass in one
   sitting. If a content doc can't be read aloud in an evening, it's too big.

## Where we sit on the spectrum

Undertale's philosophy (short, reactive, characterful, text-as-mechanic) inside a management
frame — and explicitly **never** FM press conferences. Stardew-volume is structurally
impossible under the page caps and line laws above.
